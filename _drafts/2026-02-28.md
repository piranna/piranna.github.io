Last week I've read an article that clearly resumes the current situation of
[software development in the era of GenAI](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dakharlamov_seniors-are-losing-depth-juniors-are-learning-activity-7431789557160972291-jrXf/),
and put into stats the feeling that many "software old dogs" we have: things are
not going well, and they will end up bad.

The article shows how the massive adoption of AI generated code (several sources
say 41% of newly written code is generated by AI) is leading to increased rates
of duplicated code, and plummeting code refactors since nobody see the pitfalls
that need to be rewritten since Seniors doesn't have contact with the code
anymore, just only reviewing AI generated PRs. Some years ago it was said that
for each new 1000 lines of code were written, a Senior developer would add 4-5
new bugs (a more inexperienced developer would add more), so with the actual
rates of increasing code base sizes, we are also increasing the complexity of
the code, the number of bugs, and the attack surface of the software, and it
doesn't seems that the pattern at the industry will change soon, but instead
they will double down it. Only solutions seems to be to have proper architecture
design and know-how, but in projects-oriented software development market
targeting reduced costs like the Spain one, that imply a change in paradigm (and
culture) to a product-oriented mindset, and a change in the way we do software
development, that seems to be far away from happening.

But of all the quotes in the article, there's one that especially resonated with
me the most, and I think it perfectly summarizes the situation we are living in:

> An MIT professor called AI "a brand new credit card that lets us accumulate
> technical debt in ways we were never able to before." That credit card now
> writes 41% of the code.

As somebody that has been involved in the past in several dialectical fights
regarding if technical debt is acceptable or not, being always personally
positioned in a zero-debts policy (both technical and financial), while others
have saw it as a way to buy time and get things done with the promise of paying
the technical debt and their time interests later (spoiler: they never do), I
must to admit it: **I love this one**. Just only, I'm not sure if I would be
more delighted or more afraid of it. I'm really looking forward to see how much
companies would bankrupt by the end of the year because they have accumulated so
much technical debt that they can't pay it anymore (employees will end up
burn-out due to all the extra hours they will need to dedicate to fix the issues
generated by the AI... as AI-related companies are
[already promoting 996 schedules to "keep the pace"](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cvgn2k285ypo)),
or being afraid of the economical crisis that will happens after so much tech
firms will shut-down and close doors after New Year Eve.

I have read recently about AI doesn't reduce workload but intensifies it, and
it's true: I'm not working less hours, but I'm producing like 10x more code. And
yes, although trying to have a "Product Manager" or "Architect level" mindset,
reviewing all that generated code trying to find flaws or patterns, or reviewing
1000+ sloc PRs, it's really tyring and annoying... Several studies showed that
average code reading speed is around 1000 lines of code per hour (about 4
seconds per line, or about 80 lines or a full page of code in 5 minutes). This
means that reviewing a 1000+ sloc PR would take around an hour, and that's if
you are fully focused and not distracted by anything else. In reality, it's easy
to lose concentration and overlook subtle bugs, especially when the code is
generated by AI and may have an average quality that doesn't shines for its
geniusness and keep you hooked like a good book trying to learn and awesome
yourself from it, and also doesn't keep you alert due to it bad quality and lack
of creativity so you can keep thinking on better ways to write it, so you end up
putting on cruise control and just skim through it looking for obvious pitfalls,
which is really bad for code review and can lead to missing subtle important
issues. It's true, somewhat AI generates well written code and with tests
(thanks godness! I've **never** seen so much high code coverage rates reviewing
PRs than in the last two months :-D ), but usually quality is low (AI has
[not invented here syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_invented_here)
and doesn't likes to use external libraries for almost anything), and after some
minutes to review the PR, it's easy to lost concentration and overlook subtle
bugs. We would need an AI to review the PRs generated by the AI, but _who watch
the watchmen?_

Internet critic systems are already rejecting AI generated code, and some
companies are already banning it. Also Linus Torvalds has complained that
[AI is being used to increase technical debt instead of fighting it](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/linus-torvalds-reckons-ai-is-90-percent-marketing-and-10-percent-reality),
and that seems nobody is listening. On a personal note, a couple of years ago I
had the chance of reading _Jurassic Park_ by Michael Crichton, and I was
frightened just by reading the prologue, because the "collective madness" that
shows related to genetic engineering and the consequences of it, is really
similar to the current situation with AI. We are inmersed in a "corporate"
collective madness regarding AI (with a sub-par technological advancement called
LLMs that sometimes produces useful results but
[should have not been considered anything more than just a statistical curiosity](https://tomaszs2.medium.com/richard-stallman-exposes-the-ai-lie-he-calls-it-pretend-intelligence-0cec84e8b6c1),
by the way), and we are not seeing the consequences of it, just inyecting and
burning more money into it and idolatring it as a pagan god the same way it was
done before with cryptocoins and NFTs (just only now, since LLMs can produce
_something_, they are not being seeing as bad), but they will be there, and they
will be bad.

I'm no stranger to the idiosyncrasies of writing this article in VSCode with the
help of GitHub Copilot's autocomplete or Google Translate (which also uses AI),
just as most of the recent articles I've written have been with the help of
ChatGPT: I give it ideas and even discuss them with him (it? I don't know why, I
always use masculine pronouns with "_him_", and in fact, I think I do the same
with any other AI... I think that would be a topic for another article). It also
provides some notes on the text and tone, and sometimes it even helps me with
research or reasoning about them (like an echo chamber or brainstorming session,
or for finding counter-arguments, it's ideal. It tends to be condescending and
verbose by design, and it likes to hear itself read, although I've almost
accepted it as part of its personality and don't make a big deal out of it), and
then it generates the article text ready for review (it's always good to do some
editing before publishing any text that an AI generates)... ironically, this
review was also done with the help again of GitHub's Copilot.

The thing is, this past week I ran out of credits on GitHub Copilot, and having
to go back to doing things manually or with GPT-5 (or even the idiosyncratic
GPT-4o when the free server returned a 502 error) instead of GPT-5.2 Codex Max,
my productivity has plummeted. At least I managed to get my work done
successfully, but it took more than 10 frustrating hours for what I had gotten
used to doing in 1, and it shows that I've become completely dependent on a tool
(Copilot). Me, who has always criticized languages ​​like Java or C# that, due
to their complexity (and verbosity), practically force you to use intelligent
IDEs like NetBeans that do everything for you.
